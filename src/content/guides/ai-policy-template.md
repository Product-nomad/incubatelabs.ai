# Free AI policy template for small businesses

**Last reviewed: 16 October 2026.** Practical guidance, not legal advice.

Your team is probably using AI already. Microsoft and Censuswide found 71% of UK workers have used AI tools their employer never approved. That's not a discipline problem. It means people have found something useful and nobody has told them where the lines are.

A good AI policy does that in one or two pages. This one is written for a business of 5 to 50 people, in plain English, and it covers what the EU AI Act and UK data protection law expect. Copy it, fill in the bits in square brackets, and talk it through with your team.

## How to use it

1. **Fill in the approved tools table first.** Ask your team what they actually use before you decide. You'll get a more honest list.
2. **Keep the data rules exactly as strict as they are.** They're the part that protects you most.
3. **Go through it with your team in person.** A policy nobody has read doesn't count as training. Running through it together does, as long as you record it. See [AI literacy training for staff](/guides/ai-literacy-training-staff/).
4. **Review it every six months**, or whenever you add a new tool.

## The template

### [Business name] AI use policy

**Owner:** [Name, role]
**Approved on:** [Date]
**Next review:** [Date, six months on]

#### 1. Why we have this policy

We use AI tools to save time on routine work. This policy sets out which tools we use, what we can and can't put into them, and how we check what they produce, so we protect our customers, our staff and the business.

It applies to everyone who works for [Business name], including contractors and temporary staff, on any device used for work.

#### 2. Approved tools (our AI register)

Only use the tools listed here for work. If a tool isn't on the list, ask [Name] before using it.

| Tool | Account type | What it's used for | What data goes in | Who's responsible | Not approved for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot] | [Business account] | [Drafting emails and documents, summarising meetings] | [Our own emails and documents, no special category data] | [e.g. Office manager] | [Anything listed in section 3] |
| [e.g. ChatGPT Team] | [Business account, training on our data switched off] | [Drafting, research, rewording] | [Public information and our own non-confidential text] | [Name] | [Customer personal data] |
| [Tool] | [Account] | [Use] | [Data] | [Name] | [Limits] |

This table is also your AI register. If a customer asks what AI you use, this is the answer.

Don't use personal AI accounts for work.

#### 3. What never goes into an AI tool

Unless a tool is specifically approved for it in section 2, never put in:

- personal information about customers, clients or staff (names with contact details, addresses, health information, anything from an HR file)
- confidential client or supplier information, contracts or pricing
- passwords, access codes or bank and card details
- anything we've agreed to keep confidential

If you're unsure, leave it out or ask [Name].

#### 4. Checking what AI produces

AI tools make mistakes and sound confident while doing it. Whoever sends or publishes something is responsible for it, whether or not AI helped.

- Check facts, figures, names, dates and prices before anything leaves the business.
- Don't send customers or clients anything a person hasn't read.
- If something looks wrong, it probably is. Check it against the original source.

#### 5. Decisions about people

AI can help prepare information, but a person makes every decision about people, including hiring, pay, performance, discipline, credit and refunds. That person must understand the decision and be able to change it.

#### 6. Being open about AI

- Any chatbot or AI assistant we run must tell people they're talking to AI at the start.
- We label AI-generated images, audio or video that could be mistaken for real people, places or events.
- If a customer asks whether AI was used, we tell them honestly.

#### 7. New tools and features

Before using a new AI tool, or a new AI feature in a tool we already use, ask [Name]. We'll check where the data goes, whether the provider trains on it, and whether the tool belongs in section 2.

#### 8. Training

Everyone who uses AI at work takes part in a short training session when they join and when we add a significant new tool. [Name] keeps a record of who attended, when, and what was covered.

#### 9. When something goes wrong

If personal or confidential information goes into a tool it shouldn't have, or AI output caused a mistake that reached a customer, tell [Name] the same day. Nobody gets into trouble for reporting a mistake quickly. If personal data is involved, we'll decide whether it needs reporting to the ICO, which must happen within 72 hours of becoming aware of it.

#### 10. Review

[Name] reviews this policy every six months, or sooner if our tools or the law change.

## Why it's written this way

- **Approved tools, not banned tools.** A list of what's allowed is easier to follow and keep up to date than a list of everything that isn't.
- **Business accounts only.** Business plans usually let you switch off training on your data and give you control when someone leaves.
- **A person on every decision about people.** That keeps you within the UK's rules on automated decisions and well clear of the EU's high-risk category.
- **Disclosure built in.** The EU AI Act's transparency rules have applied since 2 August 2026. See [what your chatbot must say](/guides/eu-ai-act-article-50-chatbots/).
- **Training with a record.** Since July 2026, the EU AI Act asks businesses in scope to take measures to support AI literacy. A recorded session is the simplest evidence that you have.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do small businesses need an AI policy?**
There's no law that says "you must have an AI policy". But if your team uses AI, a policy is the simplest way to protect customer data, meet the AI literacy expectations in the EU AI Act, and show you've thought about it.

**What should an AI policy include?**
Which tools are approved, what data must never go into them, how output gets checked, who makes decisions about people, how you're open about AI use, training, and what to do when something goes wrong.

**Can staff use ChatGPT at work?**
They can if you approve it, ideally on a business account with training on your data switched off, and within clear rules on what they put into it.

**What is shadow AI?**
AI tools staff use for work without the business knowing or approving, usually on personal accounts. It's common and it's a data risk, because nobody controls where the information goes.
