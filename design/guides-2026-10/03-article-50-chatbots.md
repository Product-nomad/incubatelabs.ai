# EU AI Act Article 50: what your chatbot must tell people

**Last reviewed: 12 October 2026.** Practical guidance, not legal advice.

Since 2 August 2026, anyone talking to an AI chatbot covered by the EU AI Act must be told it's AI. If your website has a chat assistant and any of your visitors are in the EU, this is the rule most likely to apply to you. It's also the easiest one to get right.

Not sure the EU rules reach you at all? Start with [Does the EU AI Act apply to your UK business?](/guides/eu-ai-act-uk/)

## What Article 50 covers

Article 50 is the Act's transparency rule. It has four parts:

1. **AI that talks to people:** chatbots, AI phone agents and AI email assistants must make clear people are dealing with AI, unless that's obvious.
2. **AI-generated content:** tools that generate images, audio, video or text must mark their output in a machine-readable way.
3. **Emotion recognition and biometric categorisation:** if you use these, you must tell the people they're used on.
4. **Deepfakes and AI text on public-interest matters:** AI images, audio or video that look like real people, places or events must be labelled. AI-generated text published to inform the public about matters of public interest must be disclosed, unless a person has reviewed it and takes editorial responsibility.

## Who's responsible: you or the tool maker?

The Act splits duties between **providers** (who build an AI system, or have one built and put it into use under their own name) and **deployers** (who use one at work).

- **Off-the-shelf chatbot widget:** the vendor is the provider and the disclosure duty sits mainly with them. But the chatbot is on your website, talking to your customers, so check it says it's AI. If it doesn't, switch the setting on or change the opening message.
- **Chatbot built for you and run under your brand:** you may well be the provider. Then the disclosure duty is yours.
- **Generated images or video you publish:** labelling deepfakes is the deployer's duty, which means you.

Either way the practical answer is the same: make sure people know.

## What a good disclosure looks like

The rules say a disclosure must be clear and distinguishable, given at the latest at the first interaction, and noticeable and easy to understand. A line in your privacy policy isn't enough.

**For a website chatbot, put it in the first message:**

> Hi, I'm the [Business name] assistant. I'm an AI, so I can get things wrong. Ask for a person at any time.

That one line does three jobs: it meets the rule, sets expectations, and gives people a way out to a human, which is good service anyway.

**Other places it matters:**

- **AI phone agents:** say it in the greeting, before anything else.
- **AI-drafted emails sent automatically:** add a short line such as "This reply was drafted by AI and checked by our team", or simply have a person send them.
- **AI images or video of realistic people or events:** a visible label such as "AI-generated" on or next to the content. Hidden metadata alone isn't enough for deepfakes.

## When the "obvious" exception applies

You don't need to disclose if it's obvious to a reasonably well-informed person that they're dealing with AI. Don't rely on it. A friendly chatbot with a human name and a profile photo is exactly the case where it isn't obvious.

## Blogs, newsletters and AI-written text

AI-generated text only needs labelling when it's published to inform the public about matters of public interest. Even then, there's no duty if a person has genuinely reviewed and edited it and someone takes editorial responsibility for it.

For a small business blog, that means: have a person edit and approve every post. That's good practice anyway, because unedited AI copy reads like unedited AI copy.

## What about fines?

Breaching Article 50 can mean fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover. For SMEs, the lower of the two amounts applies. The risk for most small businesses isn't the fine, though. It's a customer who feels misled when they discover the "person" they were chatting to wasn't one.

## A five-minute Article 50 check

1. Open your website chat. Does the first message say it's AI?
2. Is there a way to reach a person?
3. Do your phone or email assistants say they're AI at the start?
4. Are realistic AI images or videos you publish labelled?
5. Does a person review and approve AI-written blog posts and newsletters?

If you answered no to any of these, it's a quick fix.

## Frequently asked questions

**When did Article 50 come into force?**
It has applied since 2 August 2026. Generative AI tools already on the market before then have until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking.

**Does my website chatbot need to say it's AI?**
Yes, if the EU AI Act applies to you and it isn't obvious. Put it in the first message.

**Do I have to label AI-written blog posts?**
Only AI text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, and not if a person has reviewed it and takes editorial responsibility. Have a person edit and approve every post.

**Does Article 50 apply to UK businesses?**
It applies when your AI is used in the EU or its output is used there, for example a chatbot that EU visitors use.
